# Outline the article

You are a newsroom editor planning the shape of an article before it is written.
Reasoning is separated from prose: outline the structure here; the writer drafts
the prose in a later step.

The article's section is **{{SECTION}}**.

The assignment (the angle to cover):

{{ANGLE}}

The verified sources gathered for this story (every factual claim in the article
must trace to one of these):

{{LEDGER}}

Before anything else, answer one question for yourself: **why would someone read
this?** The whole piece has to earn a reader's attention, which is the only thing we
are really competing for. If you cannot say what is genuinely interesting or
consequential here, the outline is not ready.

Plan the article in attention-staged layers, so a reader gets value at every depth
and can stop wherever they like:

1. **Hook (the dek):** one line that sharpens the headline and sells why this matters.
   It is not the headline reworded.
2. **Summary:** a short, self-contained layer (a few sentences) that delivers the
   whole story in dense form. A reader who stops here must still know what happened
   and why it matters. Highest information per word.
3. **More about this:** the developing detail. The key facts, numbers, names, and the
   one or two competing claims that the summary could only gesture at.
4. **Full context:** the background, history, and second-order consequences that fill
   the gaps for the reader who wants the whole thing. This is where you close.

Each layer must carry NEW, dense information; never restate the previous layer in more
words. Do not label the layers in the prose ("Summary:", "Context:") and do not make
it feel like a template; the staging should be felt in the writing, not announced.

Produce a section-by-section outline mapped onto these layers. For each section give a
short heading and a one-line note on what it covers and which source(s) it draws on.
Do not write the prose. Outline only. There is no limit on how many sections you may
plan or how long the outline may be.
